Dear Editors,

I am pleased to submit "Prime counts and prime sums from multiplication tables: a self-similar
formula system" for consideration in Mathematics Magazine.

The paper develops a fully elementary, self-contained route from a picture every reader knows —
the multiplication table — to an exact formula system for prime counts and prime sums. Reading
the table's duplicated entries carefully yields Linnik's identity geometrically; organizing the
resulting layers gives a three-line recursive system that returns exact values of pi(y) and of
sums of primes with no prime input, no primality test, and no sieve at any stage. Along the way
the paper proves a duplicated-mass theorem and a layer-birth law (dimension k of the construction
switches on exactly at 2^k), classifies the intervals on which the system is constant, and
validates the whole machine at scale — including an exact, prime-free computation of
pi(10^9) = 50,847,534. Code for every verification is openly available at
https://github.com/carlgribble-caa/prime-moments. A companion manuscript by the same author is
cited for context only; the present paper is mathematically self-contained.

On novelty, the paper is deliberately scoped: the computational route through Linnik's identity
was pioneered by Nathan McKenzie, whom the paper cites and credits; the contributions claimed are
the geometric derivation, the structural theorems, the interval taxonomy, and the verification
discipline. I believe this is exactly the kind of piece the Magazine publishes best: a fresh,
visual derivation of real mathematics, accessible to strong undergraduates, with an honest
frontier.

Disclosure of AI use: the paper was developed in an extensive collaboration with Claude
